Rog-ri

At 6:28 in the morning, Enrique flipped the sign hanging on the glass door.

The sign was attached to a string, suspended from a nail Marco, his father, had hammered into the wooden frame two years earlier. Over time, the nail had begun to lean downward, and the string looked ready to slip off.

“Should be fine,” Enrique whispered. “We've got plenty of tape.”

He began his usual opening routine: arranging the food on the shelves, turning on the coffee dispenser and slushy machine, flicking on the lights, and reaching into the narrow gap between the wall and refrigerator to press the button that illuminated its interior.

There were only four packaged cupcakes left. Enrique moved each one a little farther apart so the shelf would look full. They had been there since last month, but expiration dates had never been part of Marco's vocabulary.

“Mmmmmm!” Enrique imitated the groaning refrigerator.

Behind the curtain separating the store from the family’s living quarters, Marco stopped snoring.

Every afternoon after school, Enrique sat behind the counter doing homework while Marco walked around the store, supposedly checking the shelves. Enrique knew his father was simply avoiding customers.

One afternoon, a woman with a large paper map entered the store.

“Excuse me, how long does it take to drive out of the desert?”

Enrique looked at Marco, who busied himself rearranging cans of beans.

“Two hours, I think,” Enrique answered.

“Oh, that's not too bad. Thank you, sweetheart.”

After she left, Marco emerged from behind the shelves.

“Those Americans talk too much!”

“She just asked for directions.”

“Why can't she look at her map?”

Enrique shrugged.

That night, he opened his geography textbook to a map of the United States. The desert was a broad patch of yellow, bordered by green mountains, blue rivers, and hundreds of tiny black dots representing cities.

He put his finger on the dot closest to their store and slowly dragged it east, then north, until he reached the edge of the page.

“About two hours to Rockridge, ma'am. Just follow the highway.”

A couple had stopped by the store on their way to a hotel reservation. The man bought two bottles of water; the woman asked about the roads.

After they left, Marco approached the counter.

“Where are they going?”

“Rockridge.”

“Oh, Rogri, Rogri. What they going there for?”

“I don't know, Dad. College?”

Marco laughed. “For college? They look forty!”

Enrique didn't laugh.

Rockridge had once been a small town, but its position at the edge of the desert had turned it into a modest city of hotels, restaurants, and shopping centers. It also had a community college, where Enrique had recently enrolled a class.

Every Saturday morning he drove west for his class. Every afternoon he drove east again, back to the store.

Sometimes, on his way home, he took the exit just before the highway entering the desert. There was a gas station there, with a small diner and a motel with a sign advertising rooms for $79 a night.

He had never stayed at a hotel before. He wondered what it would be like to wake up somewhere unfamiliar, with no one expecting him to open the store.

He usually sat in the parking lot for ten minutes before driving home.

For the rest of the afternoon, he scrolled through pictures of Japan on his phone. Neon streets, mountains, crowded train stations, people eating beneath red paper lanterns.

He bookmarked a flight to Tokyo, then closed the page when he heard Marco calling for help with the refrigerator.

Enrique owns the store now

Marco still came by every morning to inspect the shelves, although arthritis had made it difficult for him to lift anything heavier than a carton of milk.

The neighborhood had changed. More tourists passed through, and a new highway exit had brought a chain gas station and a supermarket. Enrique complained about both.

“People don't know how to support small businesses anymore,” he told anyone who would listen.

One afternoon, two women entered the store holding hands. One rested her head on the other's shoulder while they browsed the drinks.

At the counter, the taller woman kissed her companion on the cheek.

“Do you know how long it takes to drive out of the desert?” she asked.

“Two hours.”

“Perfect. We should reach Rockridge before sunset.”

After they left, Enrique shook his head.

“People these days.”

His daughter Sofia was sitting behind the counter with her homework.

“What?”

“Those two women. Acting like that in public.”

Sofia looked at him.

“Girls can marry each other too, Papa.”

“I know that.”

“Papa, you're so old-fashioned.”

He returned to counting the register. Sofia went back to her homework, occasionally laughing at something on her phone.

A few minutes later, Enrique caught himself checking Sofia's screen from across the counter.

He remembered Marco doing the same thing when he was young.

Enrique stood before a small gathering of relatives, old customers, and three men from the neighborhood who had known Marco since he first arrived in America.

“My father came to this country without knowing a single word of English,” Enrique began. “He worked twelve hours a day, seven days a week. He saved every dollar he could and built a business from nothing.”

He looked down at the folded piece of paper in his hands.

“He sacrificed so much for our family. Everything he did, he did for us. He wanted us to have opportunities he never had.”

Enrique returned to his car and started the engine. He then looked down to re-read his note.

He had written about the sacrifices his father made. The long hours. The money saved. The opportunities he had given his children.

But halfway through, Enrique stopped.

He remembered the broken coffee machine Marco had refused to replace for six years. The expired cupcakes. The arguments about college, money, and whether Enrique should find a job somewhere else.

He remembered one afternoon, years ago, driving back to the store after another unsuccessful job interview. He had told Marco that he wanted to leave the store and try his luck in another city.

“Leave? And who will take care of this place?”

Enrique folded the paper and put the car in reverse.

The following morning, he opened the store at 6:28.

The refrigerator groaned. The coffee machine rattled. The sign on the door had finally fallen off its nail.

He found it lying facedown beside the entrance.

For a moment, he wanted to throw it away, along with the rusty shelves, the old machines, and everything his father had left behind.

Instead, he picked it up and set it on the counter.

Near noon, a woman entered.

“Excuse me, sir. How long does it take to drive out of the desert?”

Enrique looked at her.

“How long?”

“Yes. To get out of the desert?”

He thought about the question. About the highway, the mountains beyond Rockridge, the unfamiliar cities on maps, and the people who had passed through his store over the years.

Then he laughed, though there was nothing particularly funny about it.

“I don't know. I've been in this desert my whole life.”

The woman smiled uncertainly.

"Your whole life?"

"Two hours, ma'am."

She thanked him and walked out the door.

Enrique watched her car disappear down the highway.

He returned to the counter and picked up the sign. The string was still intact, but the nail had come loose.

"Life..." he murmured.
