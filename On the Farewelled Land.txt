# Intro

Chua pressed half his face past the rough bark, the rest of his body still fused to the tree as if it might swallow him whole. Beyond him stretched a field of reeds—a tall, whispering reed bed. It was late autumn. The stalks had turned a weary yellow-brown, brittle with age, yet their tips still held onto streaks of green, stubborn and alive.

The world lay unnaturally still.

That stillness made the cicadas sound deafening, their shrill cries cutting through the silence, abrupt and intrusive. Chua knew one of them was somewhere out there, hidden among the reeds. Waiting. Or maybe it was just him who was waiting.

His stomach twisted sharply. Twenty hours—he'd counted, in seconds. Twenty hours since he'd last eaten. The hunger had dulled into something heavier now, a slow, gnawing emptiness. It didn't matter. He wouldn't eat anytime soon. Not while they were still out there.

And they weren't leaving.

A sudden crack tore across the field.

Chua flinched but didn't dare look. His body locked in place, breath shallow, as if even the air might betray him. Thoughts spiraled wildly—fragmented, uncontrollable. The first that surfaced was him.

That man.

The one who had taken him in when the famine hollowed out his village. The one who taught him how to survive, how to hide, how not to die.

His chest tightened. His heart pounded so hard it hurt.

*What happened to him?*

The question struck like a blow, and Chua's vision wavered. A dark blot crept in from the edges, swallowing the world piece by piece until everything blurred into black.

# Chapter 1

A young Japanese soldier moved carefully through the reeds.

He tried to tread lightly, listening for the smallest disturbance—breath, movement, anything that didn't belong. Still, the dry leaves betrayed him, cracking under his boots, branches brushing and snapping against his trousers.

He stopped.

Something ahead caught his eye.

A broken reed drifted in the shallow water. At its end, a faint smear of red had dried into the fibers.

The stalk was jagged, not cleanly cut.

Bitten.

The soldier's gaze sharpened.

---

"...You little brat..."

The voice came softly, right beside Chua's ear.

He thought he opened his eyes, but there was only darkness—thick, suffocating darkness. Panic surged through him. He scrambled backward, limbs clumsy, mind flooded with the image of boots crushing leaves, closing in, closer—

But something was wrong.

The ground beneath him wasn't wet.

Not mud.

It was dry. Warm.

And his feet—

Bare.

"Oi—enough. You trying to throw yourself into the fire?"

The world snapped back.

Chua jerked upright, gasping. His heels were inches from the fire pit, heat licking at his skin.

A man crouched nearby, tossing a bundle of wild greens onto the ground. His face was rough, shadowed by exhaustion, but his eyes were sharp—alive.

"I finally found you, little bastard, some food. Next time you bite your tooth loose, I'll leave you to the Japs."

"Where were you? Where are we?"

Chua's head was spinning. He felt as though the ground beneath him were slipping away, and it took every bit of strength he had just to stay upright and make sense of his surroundings.

"Hmph." Uncle Lee chuckled. "Doesn't sound like you're hungry. Surprising, considering how long you've been asleep."

He didn't bother answering the boy's questions. What was the point?

*Where was I?* Lee thought. *Hell if I remember. Twelve lis away, perhaps.*

And *where are we?*

Not far enough to let their guard down around those Japanese bastards, and not close enough to a town with a warm meal.

Five days earlier, the Japanese had burned their entire town to the ground. Chua and Uncle Lee had managed to escape, but Chua had no idea what had happened to his parents or his little sister. Where had they gone? Were they even alive?

All he had left was this uncle.

Uncle Lee wasn't really his uncle. He was a shoemaker whose shop Chua's parents had frequented for years. Chua had been visiting the shop with them since he was four.

Lee handed a bunch of greens to the little sleeper. Chua grabbed them and began chewing. Uncle Lee had already pounded the vegetables dozens of times to soften them. They were certainly soft enough for an adult to swallow, but for an eleven-year-old boy whose gums were still bleeding, it was another matter entirely.

"Chew as much as you can, but if you want to live until tomorrow, you'd better swallow."

Before he'd even finished speaking, Lee clamped a hand over Chua's mouth as the boy puffed out his cheeks, trying to spit the greens out.

"Didn't we have some dry rations?" Chua protested, his voice muffled beneath Lee's palm.

Chua's father had been one of the few teachers within a five-kilometer radius. Together with his wife, he ran a small private school, teaching the children of families who wanted them to learn to read and write.

Growing up, Chua had never had to worry about food or clothing. Living in the wilderness, surviving on bitter greens and whatever wild game they could catch—none of it was something his family had ever prepared him for.

"I fed them to the birds, all right? And if you don't eat, I'll feed you to the birds too. We're still a few dozen kilometers from Wuhu. If you want to make it there, swallow."

*Li: Traditional Chinese measure of length. 1 Li = 500 meters.
